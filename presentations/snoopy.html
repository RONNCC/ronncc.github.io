Meet the beagle

A dog with a world
of his own.

Charlie Brown’s pet. Nobody’s ordinary dog.

The beginning

First appearance: October 4, 1950. Home: the Daisy Hill Puppy Farm, then Charlie Brown’s backyard.

The personality

Imaginative, a little vain, and gloriously self-assured. His inner life is bigger than the world around him.

The home base

A red doghouse. A rooftop nap. A typewriter. In Snoopy’s imagination, that is all the equipment he needs.

The creator

Charles M. Schulz.
“Sparky” to his friends.

An entire world, drawn by one person.

A cartoonist from Minnesota

Born November 26, 1922. A shy child who loved drawing became one of the world’s best-known cartoonists.

A real dog named Spike

Schulz’s childhood black-and-white dog helped inspire Snoopy. The name later returned as Snoopy’s brother.

A remarkably personal strip

Peanuts began in seven newspapers on October 2, 1950. Schulz kept drawing it for nearly fifty years.

By the numbers

17,897 strips.
Every one, by hand.

One artist. Nearly fifty years of observation, imagination, and very good lines.

A few ink lines. A whole range of human feelings.

The enduring economy of Schulz’s work
Fifty years of change

From pet dog
to walking daydream.

Snoopy’s inner life grew along with his silhouette.

1950s

A familiar pet

Four paws, a round head, and the beginnings of an unusually expressive dog.

1960s

A richer inner life

Walking upright, writing, dancing, and flying imaginary missions.

1970s

A cast of personas

Joe Cool. Beagle Scouts. Woodstock. The world around the doghouse expands.

1980s–90s

A settled icon

A growing family and a familiar shape that needs almost no explanation.

The design gets simpler. The character gets more complicated.

Alter egos

One beagle. Many lives.

The costume changes. The confidence stays.

The Flying Ace

Leather helmet, goggles, scarf, and a doghouse turned into an airplane.

Joe Cool

A pair of shades. A campus attitude. Absolutely no intention of going to class.

The astronaut

From an Apollo call sign to a real journey around the Moon.

The Beagle Scout

A wilderness leader with a very small, very loyal troop.

And between adventures: novelist, dancer, root-beer connoisseur, and expert napper.

Alter ego · 1965

A doghouse.
A Sopwith Camel.

Only Snoopy can see the difference.

First flight: October 10, 1965

Helmet on. Goggles down. The rooftop becomes a World War I fighter, and the Red Baron is always out there.

More than a visual joke

The Ace is heroic, lonely, dramatic, and often defeated. Snoopy plays every emotion completely straight.

Off the comics page

The Royal Guardsmen’s “Snoopy vs. the Red Baron” became a hit in 1966. The fantasy had escaped the strip.

Alter ego · 1971

Joe Cool.

Sunglasses + attitude = an instant character.

First appearance: May 27, 1971

Snoopy becomes the effortlessly cool college student who would rather lean against a wall than attend class.

The joke is how little he does

No elaborate costume. No big adventure. A pair of shades is enough to change his entire identity.

An image that travels

You do not need to know the strip to recognize the mood. That is why Joe Cool works on a shirt, a sticker, or a screen.

A very low-effort anniversary

Still cool.
Still leaning.

Since 1971.

No classes attended.
No explanation offered.

First appearance: May 27, 1971.
Some looks do not need updating.

Beyond the doghouse

A real trip
around the Moon.

The astronaut persona became something much bigger.

1968 · A safety mascot

NASA’s partnership with Snoopy grew out of the Apollo-era focus on safety after the 1967 Apollo 1 fire.

1969 · Apollo 10

The lunar module was named Snoopy. The command module was Charlie Brown. Together, they rehearsed a Moon landing.

2022 · Artemis I

A Snoopy plush flew in Orion as the zero-gravity indicator. When the dog floated, the mission was in weightlessness.

The best friend

A tiny bird.
A very big friendship.

Only Snoopy understands every chirp.

An unnamed bird, then Woodstock

Seen in the strip in 1967; named in 1970 after the music festival. Small bird, enormous namesake.

His own kind of language

Woodstock speaks in little marks. The reader does not get a translation, but Snoopy gets the message.

Always up for an adventure

Secretary, scout, confidant, and occasional passenger. His loyalty matters more than his flying skills.

Family, rewritten

“I was an only dog.”
Except he wasn’t.

Schulz grew the family as the strip grew.

A little continuity wrinkle

In 1959, Snoopy calls himself an “only dog.” By 1965, he is wondering what became of his brothers and sisters.

A little autobiography

Spike and Andy take their names from Schulz’s real dogs. The fictional family carries pieces of his own life.

And the chosen family? Woodstock and the round-headed kid who brings supper.

Small panels. Big subjects.

The funny pages
could take life seriously.

Peanuts let children carry surprisingly grown-up concerns.

Anxiety, in plain sight

Charlie Brown worries. Lucy runs a five-cent psychiatry booth. Emotional life is not an occasional “special episode.”

Representation, without a lecture

Franklin joins the strip in 1968 as a friend and classmate. Belonging is part of everyday life.

Girls who lead

Peppermint Patty arrives in 1966: a confident athlete and team manager, six years before Title IX.

Childhood illness

The 1990 special Why, Charlie Brown, Why? follows a child with leukemia and the friends trying to understand it.

Representation · 1968

Franklin belongs.

An ordinary friendship. An important moment.

July 31, 1968 · A meeting on the beach

Franklin returns Charlie Brown’s beach ball. Their friendship starts as a simple act of kindness.

A letter helped make it happen

After Martin Luther King Jr.’s assassination, teacher Harriet Glickman urged Schulz to include a Black child.

An equal, not a lesson

Franklin has friends, school, sports, and a family. Schulz gives him a place in the neighborhood, not just a message to deliver.

A wider neighborhood

Different kids.
Room for all of them.

Peanuts made individuality part of the ensemble.

Peppermint Patty & Marcie

A bold athlete and her thoughtful friend. Readers connect with their refusal to fit neatly into expectations.

José Peterson

Introduced in 1967, with Swedish and Mexican heritage: another part of the strip’s expanding neighborhood.

Janice Emmons

The child with leukemia in Why, Charlie Brown, Why? gives friends a reason to learn empathy, not just sympathy.

A holiday sound

Vince Guaraldi’s jazz score helped make A Charlie Brown Christmas instantly recognizable, even with your eyes closed.

A familiar face, everywhere

Parade balloons, books, clothing, toys, and new animated stories keep introducing Peanuts to another generation.

The Gen Z connection

The original
unbothered icon.

Old strips. Very current feelings.

A very recognizable mood

Snoopy finds joy in a nap, a snack, or a private daydream. Sometimes opting out is the fantasy.

A design made to travel

A few unmistakable lines work as well on a phone screen as they do on a newspaper page or a T-shirt.

Failure without giving up

Charlie Brown keeps trying. Snoopy keeps imagining. Both are useful ways to get through an uncertain world.

Good grief. And good night.

We’re just
living in it.
