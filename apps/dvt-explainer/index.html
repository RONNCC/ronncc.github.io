01

The 60-second version

A deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a blood clot that forms inside a deep vein — one of the veins that runs between and inside the muscles, where you can't see or feel it from the outside. A clot in the calf is called a distal or calf DVT. It's the most common place for a DVT to start, and it's the kind that is most often found by accident or after a few days of a sore, swollen calf.

It started in a valve pocket

Deep veins have one-way valves every few centimetres. Behind each leaflet is a small recess where blood swirls slowly and sits low in oxygen. That's where most venous clots begin — not in the middle of the vein, but tucked into the wall.

"Distal" is a location, not a verdict

Anything below the popliteal vein (the vein behind your knee) is distal. Anything from the popliteal vein upward is proximal, and proximal clots are the ones that cause most of the trouble. The distance between your clot's top end and that line is one of the most useful numbers in your report.

Not every calf clot behaves the same

A short clot in one muscular vein is a very different customer from an extensive clot filling several veins and reaching up toward the knee. Extent, how many veins are involved, whether it's close to the popliteal vein, whether there's an obvious trigger, and how much it's bothering you — those are the things that drive treatment.

The point of treatment is to keep it from growing

Untreated calf clots extend upward into the proximal veins in roughly 8–15% of cases followed with repeat ultrasound. That's a number you can do something about: either a course of anticoagulation, or a short period of repeat scans to catch extension early.

If you just got diagnosed, here's the honest summary

Calf DVTs are common, they're taken seriously, and they're manageable. The clot that is already sitting in your calf is not the emergency — the emergency is a clot that travels to the lungs (a pulmonary embolism), and the whole strategy is built around making sure that doesn't happen. For most calf clots, it doesn't. For an extensive one, the plan usually leans toward treating rather than watching.

Go to emergency care now — don't wait for an appointment — if you have:

  • Sudden shortness of breath, especially at rest or getting worse
  • Chest pain that's worse when you breathe in, or a feeling of pressure in the chest
  • Coughing up blood, or a new cough
  • Fainting, near-fainting, or a racing or pounding heart you can't settle
  • Feeling suddenly very unwell, clammy, or lightheaded
  • New or rapidly worsening swelling and pain in the whole leg

These can be signs that part of the clot has moved to your lungs. This is uncommon, and it is treatable — but it needs to be assessed immediately, not on Monday.

02

What a DVT actually is

Blood is supposed to clot. Without that ability, every paper cut would be a problem. The trouble starts when clotting happens inside a vessel that is still carrying blood — a thrombus. A thrombus is not a loose lump floating in the bloodstream; it is a mass that has grown from the vessel wall inward, anchored where it formed. When a piece breaks off and travels, it's called an embolus.

Venous clots are their own species, physically. An arterial clot is usually a small platelet-rich plug formed on a cracked plaque in fast-flowing, high-pressure blood. A venous clot is the opposite: a soft, gelatinous tangle of fibrin strands trapping red blood cells, grown slowly in slow-moving, low-pressure, low-oxygen blood. Fibrin is the scaffolding; the red cells are what give a fresh venous clot its dark, purple-red colour. This is why venous clots can be surprisingly long — they don't "plug" a vessel so much as fill it, like sediment building in a slow river.

Three words that keep coming up

Thrombus
The clot itself, attached where it formed.
Thrombosis
The event — the process of a clot forming inside a vessel.
Embolus
A piece that has broken free and is travelling. If it lodges in the arteries of the lung, that's a pulmonary embolism (PE). A DVT and a PE are the same disease at two different addresses — together they're called venous thromboembolism (VTE).

Why "extensive" is a word worth understanding

Clinicians describing a calf clot reach for extent, because extent is what predicts behaviour: how many veins are filled, how long the clot is, and how close its top end sits to the popliteal vein. A common rule of thumb in the guidelines is that clots longer than about 5 cm, or involving more than one vein, or sitting close to the proximal veins, carry a higher chance of extending upward. If your report says extensive, that's usually the sort of thing it means — and it's exactly why you may be on treatment rather than on a watch-and-wait plan.

03

Where it lives: your calf veins

The calf isn't drained by one vein. It's drained by a small network: three pairs of deep veins running alongside the bones (peroneal, posterior tibial, anterior tibial), plus muscular veins that thread through the calf muscles themselves (the soleal sinuses and the gastrocnemius veins). Those muscular veins are wide, floppy, and slow — and they are where a lot of calf clots start.

Because the veins are paired and there are several of them, an ultrasound report reads a bit like a list of addresses. That's why the useful questions are: which veins, how many, and how high up.

The calf muscle pump: your second heart

Every time you walk, your calf muscles squeeze those veins like a fist around a sponge. The one-way valves above the squeeze snap shut, so the blood has only one way to go: upward, toward the heart. In a relaxed calf this system holds roughly 100–150 mL of blood, and each contraction pushes 40–60% of it north. It's why your ankles don't swell to the size of your thighs by dinner. It is also why hours of stillness — a long flight, a hospital bed, a leg in a cast, or just working at a desk with your legs down — is a risk factor for clots: no squeeze, no flow, and the valve pockets fill with the slow, stale, low-oxygen blood where clots like to start. 04

What it looks and feels like

DVT symptoms are famously unhelpful, because none of them are specific. A pulled muscle, a Baker's cyst behind the knee, cellulitis, a swollen leg from vein disease, and a DVT can all produce the same short list: a calf that aches, feels tight, looks bigger than the other one, and is warm or pink in places. Nothing about the quality of the pain tells you it's a clot. That's why the diagnosis is made with an ultrasound and not by examination — and why "it doesn't hurt that much" is not a reason to skip the scan.

Roughly half of people with a documented DVT have no symptoms at all — they're found on a scan done for another reason, or a clot in the other leg turns up when one leg is being investigated. On the other hand, a big, extensive clot that fills several veins tends to make itself known: swelling that's visibly one-sided, a calf that aches when you stand or walk, skin that's stretched and shiny.

What people describe

  • A dull ache or cramp in the calf, sometimes only when standing or walking
  • A leg that feels heavy, tight, or "full"
  • One calf that is visibly bigger than the other — socks or shoes leaving a mark on one side only
  • Skin that is warm, pink, dusky, or shiny over the calf
  • Tenderness deep in the calf, along the lines of the veins
  • New, more prominent surface veins over the calf (collaterals doing extra work)
  • Low-grade fever with no obvious cause, especially after surgery

What it can be mistaken for

  • Cellulitis — redness, warmth, tenderness; usually more superficial and sharply bordered
  • Baker's cyst (a popliteal cyst that leaks or ruptures) — calf swelling, sometimes with bruising around the ankle
  • Calf muscle tear or strain — usually during a movement, with sudden pain and sometimes bruising
  • Chronic venous insufficiency — long-standing swelling, discolouration, varicose veins, often both legs
  • Lymphedema — swelling that pits less, often slower to develop

About that "Homans sign"

You may have seen it on a checklist: pain in the calf when the foot is bent upward with the knee straight, said to mean DVT. It has been taught for a century and it is neither sensitive nor specific — plenty of people with clots don't have it, plenty of people without clots do. If a clinician tests it, that's tradition, not diagnosis. The compression ultrasound is the diagnosis.

05

What it looks like on ultrasound

This is the part most people never get to see: the actual image that gave you your diagnosis. A compression ultrasound is beautifully simple. The sonographer puts the probe across a vein and presses. A normal vein is floppy — it squashes flat, and the two walls touch. A vein with a fresh clot inside it is non-compressible: it holds its shape under pressure, because the clot is now the filling. That single observation is the primary criterion for diagnosing a DVT, and everything else on the image is supporting evidence.

How to read the image

Acute clot: easy to miss

A fresh clot is hypoechoic — dark, almost the same grey as the blood around it. The giveaway is the vein's behaviour and shape: it's distended, rounder than it should be, it doesn't collapse when pressed, and colour Doppler shows little or no flow through that segment.

Chronic change: brighter, smaller, scarred

As a clot ages over weeks and months it gets more echogenic (brighter), retracts, and the vein narrows and thickens. Often flow returns around and through it — recanalisation. That's why an old, scarred vein can look alarming but isn't a new clot.

Where they look, and why it's repeated

Some centres scan the whole leg; others scan only the proximal veins and then repeat the scan in about a week, on the logic that a clot that was in the calf will declare itself by growing upward. Whole-leg scanning finds the calf clots that proximal-only scanning would miss — which is exactly why calf DVT is diagnosed more often in centres that look for it.

The numbers worth asking about, in plain language

  • Which veins, and on which side?
  • Is it occlusive? Is the vein fully blocked, or is there flow around the clot?
  • Where is the top end? How far is the highest point of the clot from the popliteal vein?
  • How long is it, and how many veins does it involve?
  • Is it new or old? Acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic?

06

Why it happened

In 1856 a German pathologist named Rudolf Virchow described the three conditions that allow a clot to form inside a vein. We still use his triangle. Almost nobody has just one of the three — clots usually happen where two or three collide. And a great many happen with no identifiable cause at all, which doctors call unprovoked. Unprovoked isn't a euphemism for "we have no idea": it's a real category, and it matters, because unprovoked clots have a higher chance of coming back.

Why sitting still is the most common story

Stasis is the arm of the triad that does most of the work in the calf. When blood isn't moving, three things happen at once in those valve pockets: clotting factors that would normally be diluted and washed away accumulate; the oxygen tension in the pocket falls (venous valves are avascular and the pocket blood is stagnant); and the low-oxygen endothelium starts behaving like injured tissue, expressing adhesion molecules that recruit white cells and tissue factor. Injury, in other words, doesn't have to be mechanical — being still long enough is itself an injury signal.

07

What could happen next

There are three futures for a calf clot, and the whole point of treatment is to steer toward the first one.

  1. It dissolves and the vein recovers most common

    Your body's own clot-dissolving system, plus the clot's slow organisation into scar, usually shrinks and recanalises it over weeks to months. Many calf clots are self-limiting. Some leave a stiffened, less compliant vein segment behind.

  2. It grows upward 8–15%

    Untreated, roughly 8–15% of calf clots extend into the proximal veins when followed with repeat ultrasound — and once a clot is proximal, it's a different disease with a real risk of embolising to the lungs. This is the outcome that monitoring or anticoagulation exists to prevent.

  3. It leaves a legacy in the vein 1 in 3 to 1 in 2

    Damaged valves and a stiffened vein wall can cause post-thrombotic syndrome: chronic swelling, aching, heaviness, skin discolouration or scaling in that leg, worse after standing. The CDC puts this at one third to one half of people after a DVT; it's more common after proximal clots and less common after small distal ones.

The journey a clot takes if it embolises

This is the mechanism that makes a leg problem a lung emergency. Follow the route, because the anatomy explains the risk: a clot has to be big enough and free enough to ride the venous current up through the body and lodge in the pulmonary arteries. Distal clots have a longer, narrower road ahead of them; that's why they're less likely to cause a big PE — and why a clot that reaches the popliteal vein or above is treated much more aggressively.

The pulmonary embolism red flags, one more time

  • Sudden shortness of breath
  • Chest pain, worse on a deep breath
  • Coughing up blood
  • Fainting, or feeling like you might
  • Heart racing or pounding, unexplained
  • Feeling desperately unwell, cold and clammy

Call emergency services. Don't drive yourself. Say out loud that you have a diagnosed DVT and are worried about a pulmonary embolism — that single sentence changes how fast you get assessed.

08

What your care team is weighing

For proximal clots the answer is settled: anticoagulate. For calf clots, the evidence is genuinely split, and good clinicians disagree. The 2021 American College of Chest Physicians guidelines suggest that a person with a calf DVT who has no severe symptoms and no risk factors for extension can be watched with repeat ultrasounds for two weeks instead of starting blood thinners. Someone with severe symptoms or with risk factors for extension should be anticoagulated. The logic is a trade-off between two harms: the risk of the clot growing (which anticoagulation reduces) against the risk of bleeding (which anticoagulation increases). Notice that "how much it hurts" is a real input into that decision, not a footnote.

The Wells score, in case you've seen it in your notes

Before anyone scans you, clinicians estimate how likely a DVT is using a checklist called the Wells score. It's not a diagnosis — it's a way of deciding whether a negative ultrasound is trustworthy, and whether a D-dimer blood test can safely rule things out. Because the plan you got was driven by it, it's worth seeing what your own picture scores.

09

What helps

Most of what helps comes down to one physical principle: keep blood moving through the deep veins, and let the calf pump do its job. Everything below that (walking, ankle exercises, elevation, hydration, compression) is a way of applying that principle. The rest is about sticking to the medical plan, because the biggest risk to you right now is not a missed ankle pump — it's a missed dose.

Things that genuinely help

  • Take the anticoagulant exactly as prescribed, at the same time each day, and don't stop early because you feel better. Feeling better is what the drug is supposed to cause.
  • Walk. Early mobilisation is safe and is one of the mainstays of treatment; guidance does not support strict bed rest for DVT. Short, frequent walks beat one long one.
  • Ankle pumps in long periods of sitting — pull your toes up toward you and point them away, ten times, every hour or so. This is a fake step for the pump.
  • Elevate the leg when you're sitting, above heart height when you can, to help the swelling drain.
  • Stay hydrated. Dehydration thickens blood and is a small, fixable contributor to stasis.
  • Move around on long journeys or when your legs are down for hours: aisle seat, walk every hour, ankle pumps, loose clothing.
  • Tell every clinician you have a DVT — before any surgery, dental work, procedure, or new medication, and if you're pregnant or planning to be.

What the evidence is murkier about

  • Compression stockings. They can reduce acute symptoms and help once post-thrombotic syndrome has developed, but the 2021 CHEST guideline suggests against using them routinely to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome, because a large trial found no reduction in its development — and no reduction in recurrence. Ask your team whether they're for your symptoms rather than for prevention.
  • How long to treat. Three months is the usual minimum when anticoagulation is chosen. Whether you continue beyond that depends on whether the clot was provoked, your bleeding risk, and your preferences — it's a decision to revisit periodically, not once.
  • How much activity is too much. Most guidance encourages early walking. Very strenuous or impact-heavy exercise in the first days is usually something people are advised to ease into. Ask what's right for your clot's size and location.

Questions worth taking to your next appointment

  1. Which veins are involved, exactly — and is it one vein or several?
  2. How far is the top of the clot from my popliteal vein?
  3. Is it occlusive (fully blocking) or is there flow around it?
  4. Is this acute, chronic, or acute on top of chronic?
  5. Am I on treatment, or on a watch-and-repeat-scan plan? When is the next scan?
  6. How long will I be on anticoagulation, and what happens at the end of that?
  7. What's my bleeding risk, and what should I do if I bleed or bruise badly?
  8. What should I do about compression, exercise, travel, and future surgery?
  9. Which symptoms mean "call an ambulance" rather than "call the clinic"?
  10. If this was unprovoked, should I be tested for a clotting disorder?

A closing note

An extensive calf clot is a real thing to be carrying around, and it is reasonable to find it unsettling — the anatomic fact that a piece of your own leg could travel to your lungs is not a comfortable one to sit with. But the machinery here is well understood and the failure modes are mostly preventable: the plan you're on exists precisely to stop the clot from extending, and the reason for the follow-up questions and repeat scans is that catching extension early is easy. Take the medication, keep walking, keep the appointments, and use this page to ask better questions.

Not medical advice. Your clinician knows your clot; this page doesn't.
